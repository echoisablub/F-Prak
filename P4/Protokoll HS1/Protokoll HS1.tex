\subsection{Radioastronomy}

Radio astronomy is the study of electromagnetic radiation in the radio wavelength range, typically from a few MHz to several hundred GHz. Compared to optical astronomy, radio waves are much less affected by interstellar dust and gas, which allows astronomers to observe cold and dense regions of space that are often hidden in the optical regime. Radio telescopes usually consist of large parabolic antennas that collect incoming radiation and direct it to a receiver. The receiver amplifies the weak signal and converts it into an electrical voltage, from which the intensity, spectral distribution, and temperature of the observed source can be determined.

A central quantity in radio astronomy is the brightness temperature. In the Rayleigh-Jeans regime, which is valid for most radio astronomical observations, the spectral intensity is approximately
\[
I_\nu \approx \frac{2k_\mathrm{B} T_\mathrm{B} \nu^2}{c^2} = \frac{2k_\mathrm{B} T_\mathrm{B}}{\lambda^2},
\] 
% aus background 2.4.3 (gl 44)
where $I_\nu$ is the spectral intensity, $k_\mathrm{B}$ is the Boltzmann constant, $T_\mathrm{B}$ is the brightness temperature, $\nu$ is the frequency, and $c$ is the speed of light. This relation shows that the observed radio signal is directly linked to the temperature of the emitting medium \cite{1}. This makes radio astronomy a powerful method for studying cold molecular clouds, ionized gas regions, and neutral hydrogen structures in the Milky Way and beyond.

One of the most important spectral lines in radio astronomy is the 21 cm line emitted by neutral hydrogen.

\subsection{The 21 cm Hydrogen Line}

The hydrogen atom consists of a proton and an electron. In the ground state, the proton and electron spins can be oriented either parallel or antiparallel to each other. This gives rise to a hyperfine splitting of the ground state: the antiparallel configuration has a slightly lower energy than the parallel configuration. A transition from the higher-energy state to the lower-energy state leads to the emission of a photon with a frequency of approximately
\[
\nu \approx 1420.4\ \mathrm{MHz},
\]
which corresponds to a wavelength of
\[
\lambda \approx 21.1\ \mathrm{cm}.
\]
This transition is therefore called the 21 cm line.

This line is of great importance in astronomy because hydrogen is the most abundant element in the interstellar medium. Neutral hydrogen emits strongly at 21 cm, allowing astronomers to map the distribution of gas in galaxies. The 21 cm line is especially useful because it is only weakly absorbed by interstellar dust, so it can reveal regions that are difficult or impossible to observe in the optical range.

The 21 cm line also enables the measurement of Doppler shifts. If the hydrogen gas is moving relative to the observer, the frequency of the emission is shifted. From this shift, the radial velocity of the gas can be determined. This makes it possible to study galactic rotation, gas dynamics, and the mass distribution of galaxies. For this reason, the 21 cm line is one of the most important tools in modern radio astronomy and is fundamental for studying neutral hydrogen in the universe.

\section{Observation and Evaluation}
\subsection{The KRT3 Telescope}
The KRT3 is a SPIDER 300A radio telescope manufactured by PrimaLuceLab SpA. It was installed in 2019 and consists of a parabolic antenna with a diameter of \qty{3}{m} and a digital receiver. The antenna is mounted on an alt-azimuth mount, allowing it to move independently in elevation and azimuth and to track astronomical sources automatically.

The antenna focuses incoming radio waves onto a receiving horn located at its primary focus. A septum inside the horn separates the radiation into left-hand circular polarization (LHCP) and right-hand circular polarization (RHCP). Each polarization is amplified by a separate low-noise amplifier located directly behind the horn. This arrangement minimizes signal losses before the first amplification stage. The low-noise amplifiers provide a gain of approximately \qty{33}{dB}.

The digital superheterodyne receiver covers a frequency range from \qty{1398}{MHz} to \qty{1460.5}{MHz}. This range includes the 21-cm hydrogen line at approximately \qty{1420.4}{MHz}. The total bandwidth is divided into 1024 channels, each with a bandwidth of approximately \qty{61}{kHz}. The channels are grouped into 16 Base Band Converters (BBCs): BBCs 1--8 record the left-hand circular polarization, while BBCs 9--16 record the right-hand circular polarization.

The received signals are mixed down to a lower intermediate frequency and digitized using a 14-bit analogue-to-digital converter. A digital signal processor then calculates the spectrum using a Fourier transform. The telescope and receiver are controlled with the software RADIOUNIVERSEPRO, which is used to point the antenna, record measurements, and display the resulting data.

\subsection{Calibration}
\subsubsection{Determination of the system temperature}
As the reciever recieves radiation not only from the source $T_S$ but also from the environment $T_h$. In addition to those two external sources of radiation, the antenna also picks up on the radiation it's own system emits $T_{sys}$. This means the measured power of the reciever, represented by the antenna temperature, can be broken down the following way:
\begin{align}
	T_A=T_S+T_h+T_{sys}
\end{align}
It is difficult to seperate the pure system temperature and the background radiation, so these temperatures are usually combined into an effective system temperature:
\begin{align}
	T'_{sys}=T_{sys}+T_h
\end{align}
The effective system temperature now includes all unwanted contributions. This means that to know the contribution of a source, one must know the effective system temperature. Determining the effective system temperature is calibrating the telescope by doing a 'On-Off' measurement through measuring a source with a known flux density and then measuring a piece of empty sky. The KRT3 however, can only measure the sun as a source with high enough flux density but the sun is not a constant source which is why a noise generator needs to be used.\\
The noise generator injects a signal directly into the antenna horn. The calibration temperature $T_{cal}=\qty{170}{K}$ is the expected antenna temperature that is observed additionally when the noise generator is switched on.\\
When assuming the sun to be a point-like source \autoref{eq:pointsrc} can be used in the form of:
\begin{align}
	T_b\Omega_s=T_A\Omega_A
\end{align}
with $T_b$ being the brightness temperature, $\Omega_s$ being the solid angle of the sun, $T_A$ being the antenna temperature and $\Omega_A$ being the solid angle of the antenna main lobe.\\
The goal is measuring the antenna temperature of the sun and converting it into a flux density. For this we need the following identities: the antenna theorem $A_e\Omega_A=\lambda^2$, the Rayleigh-Jeans law $B_\nu=2kT_b/\lambda^2$, the flux density $S_\nu=B_\nu\Omega_s$ and the aperture efficiency $\eta=A_e/A_g=0.5$ with $A_g$ being the geometric antenna surface assumed to be a circle.
\begin{align}
	T_b\Omega_s&=T_A\Omega_A\\
	\Omega_{A}=\frac{\lambda^2}{A_{e}}, \Omega_{S}&=\frac{S_{\nu}}{B_{\nu}}, B_{\nu}=\frac{2k_{B}T_{b}}{\lambda^2}\\
	S_{\nu}=\frac{T_{A}\cdot 2k_{B}}{A_{e}}&=f(T_{A},A_{e})\\
	A_{e}=\pi r^{2}\cdot\eta=1,5^2\pi\cdot0,5&=\pi\qty{1,125}{m^2}2\\
	\Rightarrow T_{A}=\frac{S_{\nu}A_{e}}{2k_{B}}&=\frac{S_{\nu}\pi\qty{1,125}{m^2}}{2k_{B}}
\end{align}

\subsection{Determination of the Sensitivity of the instrument}
\subsubsection{Theoretical sensitivity}
To determine the theoretical sensitivity of the instrument, the weakest detectable flux density $\Delta S_\nu$ for a given system temperature $T'_{sys}$ can be given by the radiometer equation:
\begin{align}
\label{eq: radiometer}
\Delta S_\nu=\frac{2k}{A_e}\frac{C_sT'_{sys}}{\sqrt{\Delta\nu\tau}}
\end{align}
where $C_s\approx 2$ is a dimensionless instrumental constant and $\tau$ the integration time. 
With \autoref{eq: radiometer} the limiting sensitivity in a single channel with $\Delta\nu=\qty{61}{kHz}$ and an integration time of $\tau=\qty{1}{s}$ can be calculated to:
\begin{align}
	\Delta S_{\nu,\text{left}} &= 6,248 \cdot 10^{-24} \frac{\text{W}}{\text{m}^2\text{Hz}} = 624,92 \text{ Jy} \\
	\Delta S_{\nu,\text{right}} &= 6,82 \cdot 10^{-24} \frac{\text{W}}{\text{m}^2\text{Hz}} = 682,02 \text{ Jy}
\end{align}
With $T'_{\text{sys,left}} = 98.76 \pm 22.54 \text{ K}$, $T'_{\text{sys,right}} = 107.80 \pm 31.65 \text{ K}$, $A_{e} = 1.125\pi$, $\tau = 1\text{s}$, $\Delta \nu = 61\text{kHz}$.

The measurement is carried out with the radio source Vir A.\\
The necessary integration time for detecting Vir A with a signal-to-noise ratio of $S/N=10$ in each channel is calcuated, taking the flux density at $\nu=\qty{1420.4}{MHz}$ to be $S_\nu=\qty{211}{Jy}\pm\qty{11}{Jy}$ after Baars et al. (1977):
\begin{itemize}
    \item Virgo A: Flux density $= 211 \pm 11 \text{ Jy}$, Radiogalaxy
    \item For 5 channels on each side ($\Delta \nu = 5 \cdot 61\text{kHz} = 305\text{kHz}$)
    \item $S/N = 10$
    \item $\tau = \left( \frac{2k\cdot C_{S}\cdot T'_{\text{sys}}}{\Delta S_{\nu}\cdot A_{E}} \right)^{2} \cdot \frac{\Delta\nu}{S/N}$
    \item $\tau_{l} = 4,12\text{s}$
    \item $\tau_{r} = 5,22\text{s}$
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Determination of the uncertainty of radiant flux measurements}
The radio signals from space recieved on earth are weak. This can be illustrated by the thought experiment of recieving the signal of a mobile radio on the moon transmitting with \qty{2}{W}, \qty{900}{MHz} and a FWHM of \qty{80}{kHz}, $\sigma=\qty{34}{kHz}$ being recieved on earth. In this scenario the flux density is:
\begin{align}
	S_{\nu} &= \frac{P}{4\pi d^2\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma} = \qty{1263.16}{Jy}
\end{align}
When compared with the flux density of Cas A at \qty{900}{MHz} ($\qty{1420}{Jy}\pm\qty{70}{Jy}$), it's surprisingly high.\\
To calculate the flux density of Cas A, we assume the spectral response is a simple power law of $S_{\nu} \propto \nu^{\alpha}, \quad \alpha = -0,7$:
$\nu = 1420.4\text{ MHz}$: $S_{\nu,\text{CasA}} = 1420 \pm 70 \text{ Jy}$.\\
Using the previous equation $S_\nu=f(T_A,A_e)$, the antenna temperature $T_A$ for Cas A can be calculated:
\begin{align}
c &= \frac{1420,4^{-0,7}\text{ MHz}}{1420\text{ Jy}} \\
S_{\nu,\text{Cas A}} &= \frac{900^{-0,7}}{c} \text{ Jy} = 1954,37 \text{ Jy} \\
T_{A} &= \frac{S_{\nu}A_{e}}{2k_{B}} = 1,82 \text{ K} \quad (\text{für } \nu = 1420.4\text{ MHz})
\end{align}

To measure the flux density means to measure the source (On) and then to do a measurements without the source (Off). As we need to separate the background from the antenna temperature, a so-called Beamswitch measurement needs to carried out in which the position of the source and a reference position next to the source are measured alternately.\\
From this On-Off measurement the temperature of the source $T_s$ can be determined the following way:
\begin{align}
	T_A(ON)-T_A(OFF)=(T_s+T'_{sys})-T'_{sys}
\label{eq:antenna onoff}
\end{align}
The source Vir A was chosen to be observed in a grid pattern on the sky where the KRT3 takes measurements line by line at differnt positions. This means different columns are scanned to assemble the grid pattern into a map in the shape of $\text{pixel} = \text{rows} \times \text{columns}$. The reason Vir A was chosen and not one of the brighter sources, was that Vir A was the only source that fit the parameters of an elevation between \qty{30}{\degree} and \qty{60}{\degree}.\\
The BBCs 3 and 11 were chosen as they showed the smallest RFI influence even though they show the H1 line.\\
The measurements were carried out in the tab Mapping with the following settings:
\begin{itemize}
	\item Width: 10 deg; Height: 10 deg
	\item Delta: 0.5 HPBW
	\item Average: \qty{10}{s}
\end{itemize}
The mapping process took 28:35.4\,s according to the software and 29:27.8\,s according to our own timer.

\subsection{Observation of neutral hydrogen in the Milky Way}
One of the approximations determining the radial velocity is the assumption that the hydrogen is distributed in the plane on circular orbits around the galactic centre. This approximation leads to the following orbital velocity:
\begin{align}
	v(R)^2=\frac{GM_R}{R}
\end{align}
With $M_R$ being the mass of the Milky Way within the radius $R$ and the gravitational constant $G=\qty{6.67e-11}{m^3\per\kg\per\s^2}$.\\\\
As we observe from a point in the same system we want to measure, we need to think about how to actually measure the variable $R$. We observe along a line of sight defined through the relative angle $l$ with respect to the line connecting the sun and the galactic centre (see \autoref{fig:angle}). This means our calculation for $R$, the distance to the point with the highest radial velocity, is $R=R_0\sin(l)$ with $R_0=\qty{8.5}{kpc}$ being the distance of the sun from the galactic centre.\\
\begin{figure}[h]
     \centering
     \includegraphics[scale=0.4]{img/angle_of_measurement.png}
    \caption{A schematic representation of the rotation of H1 clouds around the galactic centre together with the resulting line profile to the right \cite{0}.}
    \label{fig:angle}
\end{figure}
The orbital velocity at the tangential point is $v=\omega R$ with $\omega$ being the angular velocity at distance $R$. This leads to the radial velocity between the tangent point and the sun:
\begin{align}
	v_\text{rad}&=\omega(R)R-\omega_0 R_0\sin(l)\\
	&=v(R)-\omega_0R
	\label{eq:ang vel}
\end{align}
The measurement carried out is an On-Off measurement at a point on the galactic plane. The location of the galactic centre is at $l=0$, $b=0$ with $l$ being the galactic longitude and $b$ being the galactic latitude. The spectra were taken with constant $b$ and variied $l$ in the range of \qty{10}{\degree} to \qty{90}{\degree}.\\
For that, the telescope is aligned to the galactic plane using the command line in RadioUniversePro. The USBs 3 and 11 in the BBC Tools tab were selected and the following settings were used:
\begin{itemize}
	\item Repetitions: 10
	\item Average [s]: 10
	\item Elev cutoff [deg]: 60
	\item Distance [beam]: 4
	\item galoff
	\item Aquire OnOff Spectra
	\item Save as FITS
\end{itemize}

\section{Results}

\subsection{Determination of the system temperature}
The system temperature was determined from the calibration measurements using the relation
\[
T'_{\mathrm{sys}} = T_{\mathrm{cal}} \frac{P_{\mathrm{off}}}{P_{\mathrm{cal}}-P_{\mathrm{off}}},
\]
where $T_{\mathrm{cal}}$ is the calibration temperature and $P_{\mathrm{off}}$ and $P_{\mathrm{cal}}$ are the received powers with the noise source switched off and on, respectively. The resulting values were averaged separately for the left circular polarization (BBC 1--8) and the right circular polarization (BBC 9--16), yielding
\[
T'_{\mathrm{sys,left}} = 98.76 \pm 22.54\,\mathrm{K},
\qquad
T'_{\mathrm{sys,right}} = 107.80 \pm 31.65\,\mathrm{K}.
\]
Using these system temperatures, the antenna temperature of the Sun was then calculated from the corresponding calibration data, giving
\[
T_{\mathrm{sun,left}} = 1237.79 \pm 89.62\,\mathrm{K},
\qquad
T_{\mathrm{sun,right}} = 1302.59 \pm 119.16\,\mathrm{K}.
\]
The larger uncertainty in the right polarization reflects the larger scatter of the corresponding measurements. 
With \autoref{eq: antenna temp} and the current measured flux densities provided by the SWC of the Australian Gouvernment ($S_{\nu} \approxeq 76 \, \text{sfu}$) the expected antenna temperature was estimated to be
\[
T_A = 972.753\,\mathrm{K}.
\]
This value is in reasonable agreement with the measured temperature, confirming that the calibration procedure yields a consistent estimate of the solar signal.

\subsection{Background Radiation}
In order to convert the raw TPI signal into the actual background temperature, the measured data was normalized using the mean off-source power and the effective system temperature according to
\begin{equation}
    T_{h,n}=\frac{\mathrm{TPI}\cdot T'_{\mathrm{sys}}}{\bar{P}_{\mathrm{off}}}.
    \label{eq: normalization}
\end{equation}
Here, $T'_{\mathrm{sys}}$ is the effective system temperature and $\bar{P}_{\mathrm{off}}$ is the mean reference power measured during the off-source observation. This normalization removes the dependence on the receiver gain and instantaneous power level and rescales the measured TPI signal into an effective temperature. The resulting background curve (see \autoref{fig: background}) therefore represents the true elevation-dependent contribution of the environment to the antenna temperature, which must be corrected for in order to recover the source signal reliably.
\begin{figure}[H]
     \centering
     \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{img/calib/background plot.png}
    \caption{Normalised Background Temperature as a function elevation (left figure) or azimuth (right figure)}
    \label{fig: background}
\end{figure}
The background temperature $T_h$ is not constant, but varies significantly with elevation. The background plot shows a clear increase toward lower elevations, indicating that the antenna receives additional radiation from the atmosphere and surrounding environment when observing near the horizon. At higher elevations, the environmental contribution decreases and $T_h$ approaches a lower value set mainly by sky brightness and instrumental noise.

The background temperature also varies with azimuth, showing that the local environment contributes differently depending on the telescope pointing direction. This is consistent with differing amounts of ground spillover and thermal emission from nearby structures in different azimuthal directions.

The uncertainty is significantly larger for the right-hand polarization than for the left-hand polarization, especially as a function of azimuth. This suggests that the right channel is more strongly affected by instrumental systematics and environmental variations. The smaller uncertainties in the left polarization indicate a more stable response and a more reliable background determination.

Using the extrapolated course of $T'_{h}$ (see \autoref{fig: extrap_background}) up to an elevation of $0^\circ$, it becomes clear that the background temperature rises strongly at low elevations. A comparison with the Earth's effective radiation temperature, $T_{\mathrm{Earth}} \approx 252\,\mathrm{K}$, shows that this increase is of a comparable magnitude and therefore reflects the dominance of atmospheric and ground emission at low elevation.

\subsection{Determination of the Angular Resolution of the KRT3}

The angular resolution of the KRT3 was determined by scanning the Sun in longitude and latitude. The measured signal was normalized to the expected solar antenna temperature $T_{A,\mathrm{Sun}}$. The resulting beam profiles were fitted with Gaussian functions, as shown in Figures~\ref{fig:gaus_fit_left} and~\ref{fig:gaus_fit_right}. The angular resolution was obtained from the full width at half maximum (FWHM) of the fitted Gaussian profiles.
\begin{figure}[H]
     \centering
     \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{img/ang res/gaussian fit left plot.png}
    \caption{Gaussian fit of the }
    \label{fig:gaus_fit_left}
\end{figure}
\begin{figure}[H]
     \centering
     \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{img/ang res/gaussian fit right plot.png}
    \caption{Gaussian fit of the }
    \label{fig:gaus_fit_right}
\end{figure}
The resolution was determined separately for the longitude and latitude scans and for both circular polarization channels. In the longitude scans, the measured angular offset does not correspond directly to a great-circle distance on the sky. Therefore, the angular width was corrected using the factor $\cos(\mathrm{ELV})$, where $\mathrm{ELV}$ is the elevation of the Sun ($\mathrm{ELV} = 42°$):
\[
\theta_{\mathrm{lon}} =
\mathrm{FWHM}_{\mathrm{lon}}\cos(\mathrm{ELV}).
\]
For the latitude scans, no such correction is required because the scan follows a great circle approximately directly in elevation.

The theoretical angular resolution of the KRT3 is estimated using
\[
\theta_{\mathrm{theo}} \approx 1.02\frac{\lambda}{D},
\]
where $D=\qty{3}{m}$ is the antenna diameter and
$\lambda=\qty{21.1}{cm}=\qty{0.211}{m}$ is the observing wavelength. This gives
\[
\theta_{\mathrm{theo}}
\approx 1.02\frac{0.211}{3}
\approx 0.0717\,\mathrm{rad}
\approx 4.11^\circ.
\]

The measured resolutions are slightly larger than this value for all scans. For the longitude scan, the measured values are
\[
\theta_{\mathrm{lon,left}} = 4.60^\circ,
\qquad
\theta_{\mathrm{lon,right}} = 4.78^\circ,
\]
which are larger than the theoretical value by approximately $11.9\%$ and $16.3\%$, respectively. For the latitude scan, the measured resolutions are
\[
\theta_{\mathrm{lat,left}} = 4.39^\circ,
\qquad
\theta_{\mathrm{lat,right}} = 4.63^\circ,
\]
corresponding to deviations of approximately $6.8\%$ and $12.7\%$.

The measured values are therefore in reasonable agreement with the theoretical resolution. The small systematic increase can be explained by pointing uncertainties, limited sampling, atmospheric fluctuations, imperfections in the Gaussian fit, and the finite angular diameter of the Sun. The right-circular polarization gives slightly larger values than the left-circular polarization, which is consistent with its larger measurement uncertainties and less stable receiver response.

\end{align}

The background is not perfectly constant across the map. Its variation is reflected by the standard deviation of approximately \qty{15.71}{K}. This noise level is considerably larger than the approximately \qty{1}{K} expected from the theoretical sensitivity for an integration time of \qty{10}{s}. The resulting signal-to-noise ratio is approximately
\begin{align}
    \frac{S}{N} = \frac{T_s}{\sigma_T} \approx 102.6.
\end{align}
The dominant limitation is therefore not thermal receiver noise, but radio-frequency interference and variations in the receiver and amplifier response. These effects also explain why the background is not spatially uniform.

The complete mapping required about $28\,\mathrm{min}\,35.4\,\mathrm{s}$ according to the software, or $29\,\mathrm{min}\,27.8\,\mathrm{s}$ according to the independent timer. 
With an integration time of \qty{10}{s} per measurement point and a $5\times5$ map, the source was observed for approximately $250\,\mathrm{s}$. Thus, the on-source measurement accounted for about $14.1\%$ of the total measurement time.

Assuming the ideal relation $S/N\propto\sqrt{\tau}$, the number of equivalent maps required to reach $S/N=10$ can be estimated from
\begin{align}
    N_{10} = \left(\frac{10}{S/N}\right)^2.
\end{align}
Using the measured values above gives $N_{10}\approx0.01$, meaning that one map would already be sufficient in the ideal statistical case. In practice, we estimate about 25 maps for a reliable detection with $S/N=10$. This difference demonstrates that the ideal square-root scaling does not fully describe the measurement: RFI, receiver instabilities, background variations, and calibration uncertainties dominate the actual data quality.
